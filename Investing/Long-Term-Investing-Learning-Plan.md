# Long-Term Investing Learning Plan

The best way to learn investing is a mix of reading a few good books, studying real companies, and building good habits. The 16-point checklist provides a disciplined framework for long-term investing.

## 1. Build the Foundation (First 1–3 Months)

Read a few classics, slowly:

- *The Little Book of Common Sense Investing* by John Bogle. It explains the case for low-cost index funds such as VTI.
- *The Intelligent Investor* by Benjamin Graham. Start with Chapters 8 and 20, which develop concepts including market behavior and margin of safety.
- *One Up on Wall Street* by Peter Lynch. It discusses how individual investors can research businesses using observations and fundamental analysis.
- Warren Buffett's letters to Berkshire Hathaway shareholders. These provide extensive discussion of businesses, capital allocation, valuation, and long-term investing.

## 2. Learn to Read a Company

Pick one company you may want to own, such as Microsoft, and read its annual report (Form 10-K) through the SEC.

Focus initially on:

1. What the business does and how it makes money.
2. The Risk Factors section.
3. The financial statements, especially the statement of cash flows.

The first annual report may be difficult. Repetition makes the structure substantially easier to understand.

A useful analogy for someone with a DBA background: financial statements are structured information with relationships among revenue, expenses, assets, liabilities, equity, and cash flows—similar to understanding related objects in a database schema.

## 3. Learn a Few Key Ideas Well

Focus on a small number of concepts and learn them deeply:

- Compounding
- Diversification
- Fees and expense ratios
- Free cash flow
- Valuation: price compared with the economics and cash-generation capacity of the business
- The difference between market price and intrinsic value

## 4. Master Investor Behavior

Investor behavior can materially affect long-term results. Emotional reactions can lead to panic-selling during declines or chasing securities after rapid price increases.

Useful disciplines include:

- Write down why an investment was purchased.
- Define investment and valuation rules before market volatility occurs.
- Avoid allowing daily price movements to replace fundamental analysis.
- Revisit the investment thesis when facts change, not merely when price changes.

## 5. Learn by Doing, but Keep the Process Controlled

A broad-market fund such as VTI can serve as a diversified core holding, while individual-company analysis can be treated as a separate learning process.

Maintain a short investment journal for each decision. Record:

- Investment thesis
- Purchase price
- Estimated intrinsic-value range
- Margin of safety
- Expected holding period
- Principal risks
- Conditions that would invalidate the thesis

Periodically compare the original thesis with what actually happened. This helps distinguish sound analysis from favorable outcomes caused by luck.

## 6. Filter Information Sources

Treat social-media stock tips, short-term predictions, hype, and daily market noise cautiously.

Prefer primary and high-quality sources such as:

- SEC filings
- Company annual and quarterly reports
- Investor-relations disclosures
- Berkshire Hathaway shareholder letters
- Reputable independent research
- Bogleheads educational material

Primary filings should generally take precedence when verifying financial facts.

## Simple Learning Plan

- Read approximately one investing book per month.
- Study at least one company in depth each quarter.
- Read the company's 10-K before making a serious long-term investment decision.
- Maintain an investment journal.
- Review investment decisions periodically and conduct a deeper annual review.
- Separate changes in market price from changes in business value.

## Investment Framework

Use the 16-point investment yardstick before considering an individual stock purchase. After establishing a conservative, evidence-backed intrinsic-value range, apply the margin-of-safety discipline.

A falling price alone is not a buy signal. Fundamentals, valuation, risks, and the investment thesis must remain intact.
