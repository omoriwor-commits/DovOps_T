# Investment Glossary

A practical investing glossary organized from foundational concepts through company analysis, valuation, and risk management. Market-dependent figures should always be verified with current data before making an investment decision.

## Level 1: The Basics

- **Stock (share):** A unit of ownership in a company.
- **Ticker:** A security's short trading symbol, such as MSFT. VTI is the ticker for an ETF rather than an individual company.
- **Portfolio:** All investments owned together.
- **Diversification:** Spreading capital across investments so that the failure or poor performance of one holding has less impact on the overall portfolio.
- **Compounding:** Earning returns on both the original investment and previously accumulated returns.
- **Dividend:** Cash or other value distributed by a company to shareholders. Visa pays a dividend; Berkshire Hathaway historically has not paid a regular cash dividend.
- **Capital gain:** The increase in value realized when an investment is sold for more than its cost basis, before considering taxes and transaction costs.
- **Market capitalization (market cap):** Share price multiplied by shares outstanding. Because prices and share counts change, market cap must be checked using dated data.

## Level 2: Buying and Selling

- **Market order:** An instruction to buy or sell promptly at the best available market price. Execution is prioritized, but the exact execution price is not guaranteed.
- **Limit order:** An instruction to buy or sell only at a specified price or better. A buy limit does not guarantee execution.
- **Day order:** An order that normally expires at the end of the trading session if it has not executed.
- **GTC (Good-'Til-Canceled):** An order that remains active until executed, canceled, or expired under the broker's rules. Always verify the broker's current expiration policy.
- **Bid:** The highest currently quoted price a buyer is willing to pay.
- **Ask:** The lowest currently quoted price a seller is willing to accept.
- **Bid-ask spread:** The difference between the bid and ask.
- **Filled:** The order has executed. A fill may be complete or partial.
- **Volatility:** The magnitude and frequency of changes in an investment's price or returns.
- **Bull market / bear market:** Common market terminology for sustained advances and declines. A decline of roughly 20% from a recent high is a commonly used convention for describing a bear market, not a universal legal or technical definition.

## Level 3: Funds

- **Index:** A rules-based measure representing a specified market or group of securities, such as the S&P 500.
- **Index fund:** A fund designed to track an index.
- **ETF (exchange-traded fund):** A pooled investment vehicle whose shares generally trade on an exchange throughout the trading day.
- **Mutual fund:** A pooled investment vehicle generally transacted at its calculated end-of-day net asset value rather than continuously throughout the trading session.
- **Expense ratio:** Annual fund operating expenses expressed as a percentage of fund assets. Verify the current expense ratio in the fund sponsor's latest materials.
- **Asset allocation:** How a portfolio is divided among asset classes such as equities, fixed income, and cash.
- **Rebalancing:** Adjusting holdings periodically to restore a portfolio's intended allocation.
- **Dollar-cost averaging (DCA):** Investing a predetermined amount at regular intervals regardless of short-term market prices.

## Level 4: Reading a Company — The 16-Point Yardstick

- **Revenue:** Money generated from business operations before expenses; commonly called the top line.
- **Net income (earnings):** Accounting profit after recognized expenses, interest, and taxes.
- **EPS (earnings per share):** Earnings attributable to common shareholders divided by the applicable weighted-average share count. Basic and diluted EPS can differ.
- **Margin:** A measure of profitability relative to revenue. Important examples include gross margin, operating margin, and net margin.
- **Free cash flow (FCF):** Cash generated by operations after capital expenditures under a commonly used formulation. Definitions can vary, so calculations should state exactly what is included.
- **Balance sheet:** A financial statement showing assets, liabilities, and shareholders' equity at a particular date.
- **Debt-to-EBITDA:** A leverage ratio comparing debt with earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortization. It is not literally the number of years required to repay debt and should be interpreted with cash flow, interest expense, liquidity, and debt maturities.
- **ROIC (return on invested capital):** A measure of how efficiently a business generates operating returns from the capital invested in it. Calculation methodologies vary.
- **Moat:** A durable competitive advantage, potentially arising from network effects, switching costs, brand strength, intellectual property, scale, regulation, or cost advantages. Visa's payment network is an example worth analyzing for network effects.
- **Dilution:** An increase in shares outstanding that reduces an existing shareholder's percentage ownership unless offset by repurchases or additional purchases.
- **Share buyback:** A company repurchasing its shares. Buybacks can increase remaining shareholders' proportional ownership when shares are actually retired or held as treasury stock and net share count falls, but value creation depends heavily on the repurchase price and financing.
- **10-K:** A U.S. public company's comprehensive annual filing with the SEC.
- **10-Q:** A quarterly SEC filing for applicable U.S. public companies.
- **8-K:** A current report used to disclose specified material events.

## Level 5: Valuation — Price vs. Value

- **P/E ratio:** Market price per share divided by earnings per share. It represents how much investors are paying for each dollar of reported earnings. P/E ratios are time-sensitive and should be calculated using clearly identified earnings periods.
- **Earnings yield:** Earnings divided by market price, mathematically the inverse of the P/E ratio when the same earnings measure is used. It can provide one reference point for comparison with fixed-income yields, but the risks are fundamentally different.
- **Intrinsic value:** An estimate of the economic value of an investment based on assumptions about future cash flows, growth, risk, and required returns. It is an estimate, not an observable fact.
- **DCF (discounted cash flow):** A valuation method that estimates present value by forecasting future cash flows and discounting them using an appropriate required rate of return.
- **Book value:** Assets minus liabilities under accounting rules. Its usefulness varies significantly by industry and business model. It has historically been relevant to analysis of Berkshire Hathaway, but it should not be used alone.
- **Margin of safety:** The difference between estimated intrinsic value and the purchase price, providing a buffer against valuation errors, adverse developments, and uncertainty.

### 25% Margin-of-Safety Discipline

For individual-stock opportunities in this framework:

1. Analyze the business using the full 16-point yardstick.
2. Establish a conservative, evidence-backed intrinsic-value **range**.
3. Use the **lower end** of that range as the reference point.
4. Require the current market price to be at least **25% below** that lower value before triggering the strict opportunity threshold.
5. Confirm that the underlying fundamentals and investment thesis remain intact.

A falling share price alone is **not** a buy signal.

## Level 6: Risk and the Bigger Picture

- **Risk tolerance:** An investor's financial and behavioral ability to withstand losses and volatility without abandoning the investment plan.
- **Inflation:** The rate at which the general price level rises, reducing purchasing power.
- **Real return:** Investment return adjusted for inflation. A simple approximation is nominal return minus inflation; the exact calculation is \((1+r)/(1+i)-1\).
- **Interest rates:** The cost of borrowing and return available on lending. Changes in rates can affect equity valuations, bond prices, borrowing costs, and economic activity.
- **Recession:** A significant, broad-based decline in economic activity. Formal definitions and dating methodologies vary.
- **Concentration risk:** Exposure created when too much of a portfolio depends on one company, industry, factor, geography, or correlated group of investments.
- **Opportunity cost:** The value of the best alternative forgone when capital is committed to one investment rather than another.

## How to Learn These Concepts

Do not try to memorize the glossary at once. Work through one level every week or two and apply each concept to real financial statements and investments.

For company analysis, start with SEC filings and company investor-relations materials. For example, when studying Microsoft:

1. Locate its latest 10-K.
2. Identify revenue and operating income.
3. Review the cash-flow statement and calculate or reconcile free cash flow.
4. Examine cash, debt, and debt maturities.
5. Review diluted shares outstanding for dilution.
6. Read material risk disclosures.
7. Estimate a conservative valuation range.
8. Compare market price with the valuation range only after completing the fundamental analysis.

## Core Principle

> **Price tells you what the market is asking. Fundamental analysis helps estimate what the investment may be worth. A lower price is useful only when value and the investment thesis remain intact.**
